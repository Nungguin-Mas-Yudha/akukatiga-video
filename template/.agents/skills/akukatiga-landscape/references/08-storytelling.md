# Storytelling: a story that teaches the science

## Goal

The viewer leaves understanding the science well enough to explain why the problem was hard, and feeling the person
who solved it. Biography alone is a Wikipedia page read aloud; science alone is a lecture. The film braids them so
that each needs the other.

## Where this comes from

Studied 2026-10-30 from the transcripts of two Veritasium films: *Why It Was Almost Impossible to Make the Blue LED*
(33:45, 56.8M views) and *The Man Who Accidentally Killed The Most People In History* (24:57, 43.5M views). Their
structure is taken, never their words or facts.

## The pattern

1. **Open on an object and a surprise (first 30-60 s).** Something the viewer owns or has seen, and one fact about it
   they did not know. Then the stakes in one breath: what the world would have if the problem were solved. Then the
   impossibility: who tried, for how long, how much it cost, and that it failed.
2. **Promise a count.** Introduce the hero by what they will do, with a number: "one person who ignored the whole
   industry and made three breakthroughs." The number is the film's roadmap; the viewer waits for each one.
3. **The underdog in specifics, not adjectives.** The size of the lab, the money (with the share of profit it was),
   the hostile boss, the machine they built themselves, the order to quit. Every hardship is a fact with a number or
   a scene.
4. **Science just in time.** The first deep explanation comes right after the stakes are clear and before the first
   breakthrough, and it runs long (five or six minutes is fine) because the viewer now wants it. It starts from the
   smallest true thing (an atom, an electron) and climbs one step at a time, each step a picture, until it reaches
   *exactly why this problem was hard*. It ends by pointing back to the story.
5. **One analogy, kept.** One physical picture for the whole mechanism (seats in a stadium, a balcony), extended as
   the explanation climbs, never swapped for another.
6. **Turn the problem into hurdles.** After the science, list the requirements that stood in the way, each with a
   measurable number (defects per square centimetre, a percentage mismatch, a power threshold). These hurdles are the
   chapters that follow.
7. **Each hurdle is a small film.** The hurdle; why the obvious approach failed; the hero's idea; a second, shorter
   piece of science explaining *why the idea works* (not only that it worked); the result as a number.
8. **Pressure alongside the science.** While the experiments go on, the human stakes rise: the boss, the money, the
   rivals. Rivals are real people given their credit, and the race between them is part of the drama.
9. **The hero's own words.** Quotes from their writing or interviews at the turning points, and their own voice at
   the payoff when footage exists (lane 4).
10. **Payoff in numbers and reactions.** The result measured, how much better than before, what others said.
11. **The sting.** What the hero got against what the invention earned. Stated plainly, sourced, without outrage.
12. **Back to the viewer.** Where the invention is in their life right now ("while you watch this on a phone"), with
    two or three numbers of scale.
13. **The prize late and brief.** Recognition is a beat near the end, not the climax.
14. **A small human ending.** A callback to something personal planted early, then the lesson in one sentence.

A second shape (the Midgley film) braids two people: the one who caused a problem and the one who uncovered it,
cutting between them, each scene a step closer to their collision. It suits stories with a rival or a reckoning.

## Proportions (Blue LED film)

About a third of the running time is science; it comes in one long block (the mechanism) and four short ones (one
per breakthrough). Story and science alternate every 2-4 minutes. No science block is decoration: each answers a
question the story just raised.

## Applying it

- In `treatment.md`, write the opening surprise, the count promised, the hurdles with their numbers, and for each
  science block the question it answers and the analogy it uses.
- Science claims are facts like any other: each step of a mechanism needs its row in `sources.md` (a review paper,
  a textbook, the Royal Swedish Academy's scientific background, a university page). A simplification is fine; a
  wrong picture is not.
- The code-drawn mechanism scenes (`full-visual/references/02-sentence-to-picture.md`) are where this format beats
  a talking head: each step of the climb is one drawn state, changing on its word.
