# The script recipe

Our own recipe for a Before the Prize script, written 2026-10-30 from a study of Veritasium: script statistics over
122 long-form transcripts (8 minutes or longer) and full beat maps of 24 of the most-viewed, read by four Opus
reviewers (history stories, engineering stories, concept explainers, physics explainers). We took structure and
craft, never words or facts. `07-storytelling.md` is the short version; this is the working recipe.

## The measured voice (medians of 122 transcripts)

| | Veritasium | Target for us | Our first Goodenough draft |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speaking rate | 160 words a minute | 150-160 | (not voiced yet) |
| Words for the film | about 3,500 | 3,000-3,800 for 18-24 min | 2,740 (too short) |
| Median sentence | 15 words | 12-16 | 9 (too clipped) |
| Sentences of 8 words or fewer | 24 % | 20-30 % | 48 % (too many) |
| "You / your" per 1,000 words | 17 (19 in the top quarter) | 15-20 | 9 (too few) |
| Questions per 1,000 words | 4 | 3-5 | 0.7 (almost none) |
| "But / yet / however" per 1,000 words | 8 | 7-9 | 5 |
| Numbers per 1,000 words | 10 (11 in the top quarter) | 9-12, each with a comparison | high (fine) |
| Opening minute with a number | 81 % of films | always | yes |
| Opening minute speaking to "you" | 76 % | always | yes |

What the numbers say: their voice is a conversation, not a chant. Sentences run long enough to carry a thought,
with a short punch after a long setup about one time in four. They talk to the viewer, ask questions and answer them
at once, and turn on "but" every few lines.

## The skeleton (a discovery or underdog film, 18-25 minutes)

| Share of running time | Move | What it must contain |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 % (first 60-90 s) | **Cold open** | An object or a scene in the present tense ("This is..."), often from the middle or end of the story, not its start. Then a surprise or a paradox, a stake with a number made physical, and the promise: one big question or a counted list. The subject named before 1:30 |
| 5-15 % | **Setup** | The hero drawn by one or two concrete acts (never adjectives); the world they are in; the wall everyone hit |
| 15-35 % | **The long explanation** | The first big science block, 3-6 minutes, triggered by a question the story has just made urgent. It climbs from the smallest familiar thing to exactly why the problem was hard |
| 35-60 % | **The quest** | The hurdles named with numbers, attacked one at a time. Each: the hurdle, why the obvious fix failed, the idea, a 30-90 s explanation of why it works, the result as a number. A turn every 2-4 minutes |
| ~60 % | **The low point** | A setback, betrayal or order to stop. The pressure of people and money, running alongside the science |
| 75-80 % | **The climax** | The breakthrough or the near miss; often a deadline |
| 80-90 % | **The cost** | Triumph followed by an ironic price (the tiny bonus, the signed-away rights, praise then doubt) |
| last 10-15 % | **Coda** | Impact numbers in the viewer's life; a smaller second mystery paid off if one was planted; recognition late and brief; a callback to the opening image or line; two to four sentences of first-person reflection |

Other shapes, when the story calls for them: a detective case (suspects ruled out one by one), a braid of two people
(the one who caused it and the one who found out), an engineering thriller (a clock, odds, a dilemma stated up
front). The skeleton's proportions still hold.

## The devices, and where they go

1. **The promise.** One per film: a counted list ("three breakthroughs") or one explicit question. Planted in the
   first 90 seconds, ticked off visibly, and the viewer always knows how many are left.
2. **Open loops.** Plant one in the first 2-4 minutes and pay it at 45-70 % of the running time. Plant a smaller one
   in the middle for the coda. Every section ends by re-arming the big question ("but that still did not explain...").
3. **Numbers as plot.** A threshold set early and revisited (a target, a count that climbs, a limit that is broken).
   Contrast pairs (500 people against 5). Every number arrives with a comparison in the same breath.
4. **Resistance.** Someone says no, in a named voice or a quoted line, at least twice: the industry, the boss, the
   rival, the sceptic. Rivals get their credit; the race between them is drama, not villainy.
5. **The viewer's voice.** Say the objection they are thinking ("You might think...", "So why not just..."), then
   answer it. Ask them to guess before a reveal. Use "you" to put them inside the problem ("imagine you are...").
6. **Turns.** Every 2-4 minutes: a reveal, a reversal, a wrong prediction, a paradox. "But", "it turns out", "and it
   gets stranger" are the hinges.
7. **People's own words.** Short quotes from their writing or interviews at the turning points; their recorded voice
   at the payoff when footage exists (lane 4). Emotion comes from them, not from the narrator's adjectives.
8. **Section stingers.** End each chapter on a one-line hook into the next (a question, a reversal, a date).
9. **Bookends.** The opening image, line or quote returns at the end carrying new meaning.
10. **Guess before the reveal.** Ask the viewer to predict before each big answer, then answer within a few
    sentences. Three or more per film, one per core science block.
11. **Demos.** Each core explanation has a moment written to be shown happening (a working experiment in code or
    licensed footage), not described. Veritasium's demos are its strongest weapon; ours are code that obeys the
    real physics.
12. **Dry humour.** A light, understated line every chapter or two, after a dense stretch. Never about the dead or
    the suffering.
13. **A second mystery.** A smaller question planted in the middle third and paid in the coda.
14. **Today and tomorrow.** The coda says where the science stands now and what is still being chased.
15. **Voices.** The person's own words, an archive clip or an interviewed expert at the two or three emotional peaks
    (a recorded expert interview, with permission, is the strongest upgrade we can make).

## Science on demand

- **Trigger.** Every explanation answers a question the story has just raised, spoken aloud ("So why was blue so
  hard?"). No explanation arrives because it is the next topic.
- **Climb.** 5-10 small steps from a familiar thing to the mechanism. Concrete case first, then the pattern, then
  the rule, then a stress test. Small worked numbers.
- **One analogy, extended.** One physical picture introduced early and stretched until it carries the thesis; small
  local analogies may join it, never replace it. An analogy follows a concrete example; it never replaces one.
- **The wrong intuition first.** State the tempting wrong idea, let it stand a moment, then break it.
- **The hardest step slows down.** 3-5 minutes; two or three angles in a row (picture, numbers, a demo or simulation);
  then a one-line recap.
- **Recap rhythm.** One line after each explanation ("So that is why..."); a fuller restatement at the payoff;
  roughly every 4-6 minutes the viewer is told where we stand.
- **Hand back.** Every explanation ends on a consequence the viewer can see (the colour, the curve, the fire), then a
  "but" reopens the story.
- **Proportion.** In a discovery film, explanation is 35-55 % of the running time: one long block early, then short
  patches exactly when the hero needs them.

## Sentence style

- Spoken, mostly 10-20 words; after a long setup, a 3-6 word punch. Never a run of clipped fragments.
- Hinges: "So...", "Now,...", "But...", "Well,...", "It turns out...", "Which brings us to...", "And that is why...".
- A rhetorical question answered at once ("So what did he do? He kept going.").
- Lists of three. Conditional stakes ("If only blue were possible...").
- Dates open sections ("In 1972,...").
- "You" to explain and to make it personal; "I" and "we" only in the reflection and asides, never as authority.
- Dry understatement for humour; never jokes about the dead or the suffering.
- Our house rules on top: no em dashes, no invented quotes, every claim with its `sources.md` row, politics out.

## Character

- Acts and objects, never adjectives: the machine they built themselves, the insult they remembered word for word,
  the order they ignored, the notebook line.
- Each person has a job in the story: the one who wants it, the wall, the doubter, the one who arrives at the
  contradiction, the one who pays the price.
- Biography enters in short bursts (20-90 s) at turning points. Tragedy and fate are kept for the end.

## The ending

Never a recap of facts. Answer what can be answered, admit what cannot, then widen: the invention in the viewer's
hand tonight, the price the hero paid, a callback to the opening, and two to four sentences in the first person that
turn the story into something the viewer keeps. Then the soft line to subscribe.

## The script checks (before the creator reads it)

1. Run the measures on the script (median sentence, short share, "you", questions, "but", numbers per 1,000 words)
   and compare with the table above. Outside the target band means a rewrite of that chapter, not a tweak.
2. List the promise, every open loop with its plant and payoff, every turn with its minute: no stretch over 4
   minutes without one. `script-check` measures the longest stretch with no question and counts the guess moments.
3. List every explanation: its trigger question, its steps, its analogy, its hand-back.
4. Read it aloud at the voice's pace: any sentence that needs a second breath is split; any run of fragments is
   joined.
5. Every fact, quote and number has its row in `sources.md`.
